Most website projects do not run late because of the building. They run late
because something the build needed was still being decided halfway through.

The pattern is consistent enough to plan around. Here is what is worth
settling before a line of code is written — almost none of it technical.

## 1. Who the site is talking to

Not a demographic. A sentence a real person would recognise themselves in:
"a factory owner in Karachi who still records stock on paper and has been
told he needs software."

This decides the writing, the order of the pages, and what the first screen
has to say. Without it, every later disagreement about wording has no way of
being settled except by preference.

## 2. The one thing a visitor should do

A website that asks for four things gets none of them. Pick the single action
that matters most — request a quote, book a call, see prices, download
something — and treat everything else as secondary.

## 3. The text

This is the one that delays projects more than any other. Design can be built
around text that does not exist yet, but it cannot be *finished* around it,
and a layout built for a 40-word paragraph breaks when 200 words arrive.

You do not need polished copy. You need the real points, in the real order,
roughly the real length.

## 4. The pictures

Decide early whether you have photographs of your own work, or whether stock
images will be used. These are not interchangeable: real photographs of real
work are the single most convincing thing on a services website, and the
layouts that suit them are not the layouts that suit stock.

If the photographs do not exist yet, that is a shoot to schedule — and it
usually takes longer than the build.

## 5. Who signs it off

One person. Not a committee, and ideally not a committee's average. Rounds of
feedback from three people who disagree produce a site that satisfies none of
them, and the cost of that shows up as weeks.

## 6. What happens after launch

Who updates the prices. Who adds a service. Who notices when a form stops
sending. A site nobody owns stops being accurate within months, and an
out-of-date site does more damage than no site.

This is worth deciding before launch, because it changes how the site should
be built — a site whose text is meant to change needs somewhere to change it
from.

## Where this leaves you

None of the above needs a developer. All of it can be settled while you are
still choosing one, and a project that starts with these answers in hand
tends to finish in roughly the time it was quoted.

If you are weighing up a build and want to talk through any of it, that
conversation costs nothing.
